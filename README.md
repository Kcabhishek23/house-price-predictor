# house_prices_10000.csv — Synthetic Finnish-Style Housing Dataset

> **This dataset is entirely synthetic.** It was generated by a Python script for
> **machine-learning education and experimentation**. It is **NOT real Finnish transaction
> data**, it does not describe real properties, and it must not be used to value, buy, sell or
> analyse any real property or market. Municipality names are real place names used only as
> category labels; no row corresponds to a real sale, listing or address.

## Files

| File | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `house_prices_10000.csv` | The dataset: 10,000 rows, 19 columns, UTF-8, comma-separated |
| `generate_house_prices_10000.py` | The generator and validator (seeded, so it reproduces the same file) |
| `house_prices.csv` | Your original 40-row file. It is unchanged and separate |

To regenerate: `python3 generate_house_prices_10000.py` (needs `numpy` and `pandas`). The random
seed is fixed (`20260919`), so the output is identical every run.

## How the data was generated

Rows were sampled feature by feature so that features depend on each other the way they roughly
would in a housing market, and then price was computed from those features plus noise.

1. **Municipality** is drawn with unequal weights (Helsinki most common, Pori least).
2. **Property type** depends on the municipality. Big cities are mostly apartments, and smaller
   cities have more detached houses.
3. **Distance to city centre** is drawn from a lognormal distribution. Houses sit further out than
   apartments, and the typical distance differs by municipality.
4. **House size** is drawn from a truncated lognormal distribution per property type.
   Out-of-range draws are redrawn, not clipped, so the bounds don't create spikes.
5. **House age** is drawn from a skewed distribution with a batch of new builds. It is scaled per
   municipality, so some municipalities have older housing stock.
6. **Condition** depends on age. Only homes 5 years old or younger can be `New`, and older homes
   are more likely to be `Fair` or `Needs renovation`.
7. **Rooms** follow floor area. Each room gets at least about 15 m² and at most 45 m² on average,
   and there are hard caps by property type.
8. **Bathrooms and toilets** grow with floor area. Toilets include those inside bathrooms.
9. **Floor** is generated for apartments only, using a hidden building height per municipality.
10. **Heating system** depends on property type, age and municipality. Oil is absent from new
    builds, wood is absent from apartments, and district heating is more likely in big cities
    and close to the centre.
11. **Energy rating** is derived from age, condition, heating system and property type, plus
    noise. Old homes are never A, and very old homes are never A or B.
12. **Parking, garden, plot, sauna, balcony/terrace and open kitchen** are drawn from
    probabilities that depend on property type, size, age, floor and municipality.
13. **Price** is computed from a set of interacting effects, then multiplied by random noise (see
    below).

### How price is built

Price is *not* one linear formula. It combines:

- **Location**: a base price per m² for each municipality, and a different price ratio between
  property types in each municipality (for example, houses are relatively cheaper next to
  apartments in Helsinki than in Pori).
- **Size**: price per m² falls slightly as homes get larger.
- **Distance to centre**: a per-municipality price decay with distance, weaker for houses,
  plus a small premium very close to the centre.
- **Age and condition**: gradual depreciation, condition multipliers, and a renovation-backlog
  cost for `Needs renovation` that scales with size. Old, central apartments in three cities
  get a small "character building" premium, so age is not monotonic for everyone.
- **Land**: for houses, plot value depends on plot size, municipality land value and distance,
  with diminishing value for very large plots.
- **Rooms, bathrooms, toilets, open kitchen**: small effects. Rooms are compared with what is
  typical for the floor area.
- **Floor**: the effect depends on a hidden building height and lift availability. Upper floors
  gain value in buildings with lifts and lose value in older walk-ups.
- **Parking**: a fixed euro value per space that varies strongly by municipality, and is much
  smaller for houses.
- **Sauna, balcony/terrace, garden**: small percentage effects that differ for apartments and
  houses.
- **Energy rating and heating system**: percentage effects, with the energy rating effect
  stronger in colder northern municipalities.
- **Noise**: multiplicative, roughly normal noise (about ±5–10% typical, larger for houses, poor
  condition and very old homes), truncated at 2.8 standard deviations. Final prices are rounded to
  whole thousand euros and clipped to 25–1,800.

The exact coefficients are in the generator script. If you want to practise recovering the
relationships from data, avoid reading `generate()` until after you have built your models.

## Assumptions

- Market shares, size, age, room, plot and heating distributions are **stylised guesses** chosen to
  look plausible. They are **not calibrated** to official Finnish statistics.
- Municipality price levels are chosen so that Helsinki and Espoo are clearly most expensive and
  Pori and Lahti clearly cheapest. The ordering is loosely realistic, but the numbers are not
  real market figures.
- Apartments are the most common type overall (about two thirds of rows).
- Features are correlated on purpose. Bigger homes have more rooms and bathrooms, newer homes have
  better energy ratings, and houses are further from the centre. This means a **linear model will
  not give every feature a positive coefficient**. Distance to the centre, for example, is
  positively correlated with price overall, because far-out rows tend to be large detached houses.
- The dataset contains a few hidden drivers that are not exported (building height and lift
  availability for apartments), so some price variation cannot be explained from the columns.

## Validation performed

The script checks these before writing the CSV (43 checks, all passing):

- Exactly 10,000 rows, with the expected columns in order
- No missing values and no duplicate rows
- Only valid category values, and binary columns contain only 0 and 1
- Numeric columns within sensible ranges (apartments at most 140 m², at most 6 rooms, price
  25–1,800 thousand euros)
- No impossible combinations, for example:
  - apartments with a plot, or houses without one
  - houses on an upper floor, or apartment gardens above the ground floor
  - more bathrooms than rooms, or fewer toilets than bathrooms
  - more than 45 m² per room (no 80 m² one-room homes)
  - `New` condition on a home older than 5 years
  - oil heating in a home under 8 years old, or wood heating in an apartment
  - an energy rating of A on a home 25 or more years old
  - a detached-house plot smaller than 2.2 times its floor area

## Suggested baseline results

Single 80/20 split with `random_state=42`, all 18 features, one-hot encoded categories.
Treat these as rough reference points, not benchmarks:

| Model | R² | MAE |
|---|---|---|
| Linear regression (as in `main.py`) | ≈ 0.85 | ≈ 33k € |
| Random forest (200 trees) | ≈ 0.94 | ≈ 20k € |
| Histogram gradient boosting | ≈ 0.97 | ≈ 15k € |

Tree-based models outperform linear regression because the price has interactions and
non-linearities (location × property type, plot value, age × condition).

## Limitations

- **Not real data.** Any model trained on it learns the assumptions built into the generator,
  not the real Finnish housing market. Do not present its results as findings about real prices.
- Real markets have effects that are missing here: time trends, inflation, interest rates,
  neighbourhood-level location, view, building quality, maintenance charges and financing
  liabilities, seller behaviour, and negotiation.
- There is no date, coordinate or address, so no time-series or geospatial work is possible.
- Location is limited to 10 municipalities, with distance as the only within-city measure.
- Noise is roughly normal and multiplicative. Real prices have heavier tails and more outliers.
- The relationships are simpler than reality. All effects are smooth and were written by hand.
- Category frequencies (for example the heating mix and the share of A-rated homes) are
  plausible-looking assumptions, not measured proportions.
- Results from this dataset are for learning and portfolio demonstration only.
